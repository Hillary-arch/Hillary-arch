Investor memo · November 2026

Emergency response is becoming a service governments and insurers pay for. Sauti is the layer that runs it.

Sauti takes an emergency call on any phone, in the caller's language, triages it with AI checked by a dispatcher, sends the nearest trained responder and alerts the receiving hospital. Every call leaves a structured record. We build national emergency response with governments, and sell to employers and health insurers alongside.

Why now

The payers already exist. What is missing is the system that turns a call into a verified response.

Governments. National emergency services are being built and paid for per response. Kenya's Social Health Authority pays KES 4,500 for each ambulance evacuation within 25 km and KES 75 per kilometre beyond, only against a verified record.2 Nigeria's national emergency medical service has grown from one territory to 34 states and is now building a national digital dispatch platform.14

Employers. Under Uganda's Workers Compensation Act, an employer owes 60 months of a worker's earnings for a death or permanent total incapacity, plus medical costs.3 A faster, documented response costs less than the claim.

Insurers. Health insurance coverage ranges from under 2% to 79% across the countries surveyed, with public schemes expanding fastest.4 Insurers pay for emergency care and need it verified.

A national contract

Three fees, sized by the country.

Sauti works with the government's own dispatch centres, ambulances and hospitals and adds what they lack: language understanding, triage, trained community responders and verified records. Governments already buy national health services this way. Ghana contracted nationwide medical drone delivery as a four-year service for USD 12.5 million, about USD 0.10 per person a year.15 In India, states fund the setup of 108 emergency centres and contract out their operation.16

The data asset

The first minutes of an emergency are the least recorded part of any health system.

Each Sauti call produces a timestamped, located, triaged case: what happened, how severe it was, who responded, how long each step took and where the patient went. It is captured as part of the response, so it costs nothing extra to collect.

The voice and triage models are trained by Sauti on its own calls, in the languages callers actually speak. Each new country adds languages and cases, which makes the next country cheaper to enter and the models harder to copy.

Data revenue is not included in the model.

What the evidence says

Coordinated first response saves lives at low cost.

Trauma

Across low and middle income countries, prehospital trauma systems were associated with a 25% relative reduction in mortality (95% CI 15 to 34%), larger in rural areas. Three of the four rural programmes used trained lay responders as the first tier.6 In Kampala, lay responders trained in one day retained and used their skills for at least six months.7

Obstetric emergencies

Tanzania's m-mama community transport system completed referrals for USD 170 each against USD 472 by ambulance alone.8 In its first year in Sengerema it was associated with a 27% fall in maternal mortality, at about USD 2,000 per maternal life saved.9 The government has since taken the programme national.17

GovernmentEmployersInsurersOperating result

Government staff answer calls in their own centres. Sauti pays community responders per completed response and carries telecom, AI inference on rented GPUs, technical staff in each centre, a country team, and the one-off training of its models in each new language.

Each country uses its own WHO road death rate, maternal mortality ratio, birth rate and life expectancy. DALYs count years of life lost; disability averted is left out, so the impact is understated. Cost is everything payers spend on Sauti in the model.

Valuation, dilution and exit multiple are yours to set. This is arithmetic on the business case above, not a statement of terms.

Contracts. A country enters in its contract year. Its contract scope grows in equal steps to the whole population over the rollout period. Technical preparation and the country team start one year before signing.

Government revenue. Setup = number of dispatch centres × fee per centre, invoiced half at signing and half a year later; centres = population ÷ people per centre, rounded up. Annual service fee = people in scope × fee per person. Per-dispatch fee = dispatches × price, where the price is a share of Kenya's per-evacuation tariff.

Emergencies. Per person per year in each country: WHO-estimated road deaths plus serious injuries (deaths × injuries per death), obstetric complications (births × complication share), and other medical emergencies. The capture rate is the share of these that reach the system.

Employers and insurers. Employers pay per worker per month. Insured members are people in scope × the country's health insurance coverage × the share whose insurer partners with Sauti; they pay a monthly fee per member, and their emergencies are billed to the insurer per verified incident instead of to government.

Costs. Per dispatch: responder payment, telecom and AI inference (GPU price × audio minutes ÷ throughput). Per centre per year: Sauti technical staff. Per country per year: country team and the community responder network (responders per 100,000 people × yearly cost per responder); plus model training for each new language at signing and the cost of delivering setup as a share of the setup fee. Per employer worker: on-site training and kits. Sales, account management and overhead: a share of revenue. Central: core team, platform and compliance, growing each year.

Impact. Deaths averted = emergencies captured × (trauma death rate × trauma mortality reduction + maternal death rate × maternal mortality reduction), country by country. DALYs = deaths averted × years of life remaining at the average age of death, discounted.

Benchmarks. GiveWell's top charities save a life for about USD 5,000.10 Under the GDP-per-capita convention, an intervention is very cost-effective below one times GDP per capita per DALY and cost-effective below three times; the chart uses the population-weighted GDP per capita of the selected countries.

Monte Carlo: where the probabilities come from. Each run decides, year by year, whether each planned contract is signed (probability per year, starting in the planned year) and whether a signed contract is later lost. The default signing probability is anchored on observed timelines: Ghana went from contract to national launch within a year, Nigeria from a one-territory pilot to 34 states in about four years, and m-mama took about seven years from pilot to national government ownership. A 35% chance per year puts the median delay at about one and a half years, with a long tail of contracts that never sign within the horizon. Each run also draws a shared market factor that moves capture, prices, employer volume and insurer uptake together, plus independent draws for responder pay, responder network cost, overhead, central costs, injury ratio, both mortality reductions and the exit multiple. The table reports the 10th, 50th and 90th percentiles of 1,000 runs. These are scenarios built on stated assumptions, not forecasts. The model does not include the risk that the company fails before its first contract, which applies to any early-stage investment.
